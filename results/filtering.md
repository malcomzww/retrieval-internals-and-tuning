Recall and empty-result rates in this file are **exact and portable**.
They are measured against the exact top-k *within the filtered subset*,
which is the only defensible reference: scoring a filtered result
against the unfiltered top-k would mark a correct answer as a miss.

## The collapse

Ask a vector index for the 10 nearest neighbours matching a filter. The
obvious implementation retrieves 10 and discards the non-matching ones.
Of 10 unfiltered neighbours, roughly `selectivity x 10` survive -- so as
the filter tightens, recall follows it down. Nothing errors.

| filter keeps | post-filter recall | over-fetch 10x | over-fetch 50x | pre-filter exact | queries returning nothing |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 50.0% | **0.490** | 0.965 | 0.965 | 1.000 | **0%** |
| 25.0% | **0.255** | 0.960 | 0.960 | 1.000 | **2%** |
| 12.5% | **0.120** | 0.922 | 0.932 | 1.000 | **27%** |
| 6.2% | **0.052** | 0.615 | 0.832 | 1.000 | **58%** |
| 3.1% | **0.020** | 0.313 | 0.683 | 1.000 | **82%** |
| 1.6% | **0.012** | 0.132 | 0.495 | 1.000 | **90%** |

From 50% selectivity to 1.6%, post-filter recall
falls from 0.490 to 0.012 -- a
**42x collapse** -- and 90% of queries
come back **empty**. The API returns `200 OK` with an empty list; no
metric that averages over successful responses will show this.

Note that recall tracks selectivity almost exactly. The collapse is not
bad luck or a badly-tuned graph: it is arithmetic, and it is therefore
reproducible on any corpus and any machine.

## Why over-fetching is a mitigation and not a fix

Fetching `k x multiplier` candidates before filtering buys headroom. The
multiplier needed to expect `k` survivors is `k / selectivity`:

| filter keeps | candidates needed for 10 survivors | fraction of this corpus |
|---|---|---|
| 50.0% | 20 | 0.1% |
| 25.0% | 40 | 0.2% |
| 12.5% | 80 | 0.4% |
| 6.2% | 161 | 0.8% |
| 3.1% | 320 | 1.6% |
| 1.6% | 639 | 3.2% |

The cost is inversely proportional to selectivity, so the filters that
need the most help are exactly the ones where help is most expensive. At
1.6% selectivity the index must return
639 candidates to expect 10
usable ones -- at which point it is doing most of an exact scan while
still not guaranteeing an answer.

## The inversion

At selectivity 3.1% and below, **pre-filtered exact
search is both perfectly accurate and no slower than the filtered ANN
search it replaces.** Restricting to matching rows first makes the
candidate set small enough that scanning it exhaustively is cheap,
while the ANN index keeps paying for a graph built over rows the query
has already excluded.

This is the crossover, and its *existence* is machine-independent even
though its exact location is not: exact-scan cost falls linearly with
the surviving row count, and the graph traversal cost does not. Below
some selectivity the exact scan must win on both axes.

It is also the core of `docs/adr/0001-when-postgres-is-enough.md`: a
relational database applies the `WHERE` clause first by default, and
so lands on the winning strategy without anyone choosing it.
